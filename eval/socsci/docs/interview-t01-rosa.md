Shift Work and Neighbourhood Life study. Interview T01 (participant P07, "Rosa", hospital nurse on rotating shifts). Interviewer: IV. Recorded 14 March 2024, transcribed verbatim. Names of people and places are changed.

IV: Thank you for agreeing to talk to me. To start, could you tell me how long you have lived on the Aldgate estate and what your work pattern looks like?

P07: I have lived on the estate for eleven years now, since 2013, in the same flat on the third floor. I work as a nurse on an acute medical ward at the general hospital, and our rota rotates every four weeks, so I do two weeks of days, then a week of lates, then a week of nights. The nights are the hard part. On a night week I sleep from about nine in the morning until three in the afternoon, and then the evening is the only bit of the day I have, and that is exactly when everything on the estate happens: the residents' association meets at seven on the first Tuesday of the month, the community garden has its work evenings on Thursdays, and the youth club needs volunteers from six. So it is not that I do not want to go. It is that the rota decides for me. When I worked only day shifts, back in 2015 and 2016, before I moved to the acute ward, I went to almost every residents' meeting, and I even took the minutes for a year. That changed completely when the rotating rota started in 2017.

IV: So would you say you felt excluded by your neighbours in the residents' association?

P07: No, not excluded, I would not put it like that at all. They always invited me, they put the minutes through my door, and the chair, Margaret, would ring me to ask how I was getting on. Nobody pushed me out. If anything they were too kind about it. It was the rota that kept me away, not the people. I want to be clear about that, because I know some of my colleagues say they feel left out where they live, but that is not my experience on this estate.

IV: How many meetings were you able to attend last year?

P07: Four, in the whole of 2023. Four out of twelve. And two of those I came straight from a day shift still in my uniform. The year before, 2022, it was even fewer, I think two, because that was the winter when the ward was so short-staffed that I was doing extra night shifts on top of my rota. I keep a diary, so I can tell you it was four last year, and I was quite proud of four, to be honest.

IV: You mentioned the community garden. What part does that play for you?

P07: The garden is the one thing I have managed to keep, and I think it is because the garden does not need you to be there at a fixed time. There is a shared rota on a whiteboard in the shed, and you can water and weed whenever you are free, at ten in the morning after a night shift if you like. I have a plot with tomatoes and beans. What I value is that I bump into people there without having planned it. I have got to know the family from the ground floor flat, the Okafors, entirely through the garden, because their boy waters on Saturday mornings. Meetings are a fixed time, the garden is not. That is the difference for someone like me. The garden also changed how I see the estate. Before, I knew the people on my landing and that was it. Now I know people from all three blocks, because the plots are mixed up and you end up sharing a hose or lending a trowel. There is an older man from block C, Mr Haddad, who taught me how to stop the slugs getting the lettuces, and in return I check his blood pressure sometimes when he asks, which I suppose is the nurse in me. None of that would have happened at a meeting, where you sit in rows and listen to the committee. In the garden you work side by side, and people talk about anything, the bins, the lifts, their grandchildren, the council. Half of what I know about what is going on on the estate I learn with my hands in the soil. When the lift in our block was broken for six weeks in 2022, it was in the garden that I heard who needed their shopping carried up, and a few of us organised it between us without any meeting at all. So when people say shift workers do not take part, I want to say that it depends what you count as taking part. If I could change one thing about the estate, it would be to make more things work the way the garden works, with a rota that you fill in yourself instead of a meeting that you either make or miss. I suggested an online noticeboard for the residents' association, so people on shifts could add comments before a meeting, and the committee agreed to try it from January this year. I have used it twice already. It is not the same as being in the room, but it means my view on the parking scheme was read out at the February meeting even though I was on nights.

IV: Has your health or energy affected any of this?

P07: Yes, honestly, more than the timing sometimes. After a run of nights I am exhausted for two days, and on those days I do not want to see anyone, even people I like. My sleep has been bad since the rota started, and my GP has talked to me about it. I would say the tiredness matters as much as the hours. Even when there is a meeting on a day I am free, if it falls just after nights, I will not go. My husband goes for both of us then, and tells me what happened, so we are not completely absent as a household.

IV: Last question. Is there anything your employer could do?

P07: A fixed rota would be the biggest thing. Some of my colleagues have been given fixed days because they have small children, and I understand that, but there is no fixed pattern for people who want to take part in their community. The hospital does give us the rota six weeks ahead now, which is better than the two weeks we had before 2021, and with six weeks I can at least see which meetings I can make and tell Margaret in advance.

IV: Thank you, that is everything I wanted to ask.
